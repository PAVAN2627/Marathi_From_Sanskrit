Abstract—Marathi evolved systematically from Old Indo-Aryan (Sanskrit) through Maharashtri Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa into New Indo-Aryan, yet no computational framework simulates this trajectory in an interpretable way. Conventional neural sequence-to-sequence models behave as black boxes, often hallucinate phonotactically illegal forms, and generalize poorly on low-resource historical corpora. This paper proposes a hybrid artificial intelligence framework that couples a rule-based engine of historical sound laws with a phoneme-level Transformer grounded in articulatory features from the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). The model uses a rule-constrained cross-attention mechanism, a contrastive objective for diachronic semantic drift, and a derivation module that outputs intermediate phonetic stages. Evaluated on a curated corpus of 12,500 aligned Sanskrit–Marathi cognate pairs drawn from classical lexicographical sources, the framework reaches a Character Error Rate of 6.12%, a Phonetic Edit Distance of 0.41, and a chrF++ score of 89.43, clearly outperforming rule-only, recurrent, and unconstrained Transformer baselines. The work contributes an interpretable paradigm for computational historical linguistics, low-resource Indic language technology, and digital preservation of linguistic heritage.
Keywords—Computational Historical Linguistics, Explainable Artificial Intelligence, Transformer Architectures, Indo-Aryan Phonology, Semantic Drift Modeling.
I. Introduction
Modern Indo-Aryan languages are among the best-documented yet least computationally formalized products of language change. Marathi, spoken by more than 80 million people and recognized as a classical language of India since 2024 [1], descends from Old Indo-Aryan (OIA, chiefly Classical and Vedic Sanskrit) through Middle Indo-Aryan (MIA) vernaculars [2]–[4]. Its lineage passes through the regional Prakrits, most prominently Maharashtri Prakrit, the administrative and poetic medium of the Satavahana period, and then through regional Apabhraṃśa varieties, before appearing as Old Marathi in epigraphic records of the eleventh and twelfth centuries and finally as Modern Marathi [2], [4]. Classical philology showed that this multi-millennial continuum followed regular sound laws [5], [6]. These include weakening and elision of intervocalic single stops, assimilation of heterogeneous consonant clusters into geminates, later degemination with compensatory lengthening of the preceding vowel, retroflexion of dental sonorants, emergence of the retroflex lateral, and deletion of word-final vowels [2]–[4].
Despite this exhaustive manual documentation, computational linguistics has largely treated language as a synchronic, static object. Contemporary Natural Language Processing (NLP) concentrates on high-resource tasks such as machine translation, classification, dialogue, and speech recognition. Self-attention Transformers [7] define the state of the art on these tasks, but applying them to diachronic reconstruction raises methodological hurdles. Standard sequence models operate on orthographic characters or statistically derived subwords such as byte-pair encodings [8]. These surface units hide articulatory and phonotactic structure and treat phonemes as arbitrary integers rather than bundles of distinctive features. Without the inductive bias of regular sound change, such models often predict phonotactically illegal segments and generalize poorly from small etymological datasets [9].
A second deficiency is opacity. In historical linguistics, the validity of a derivation rests on procedural traceability. A reconstruction cannot merely assert that Sanskrit hasta yields Marathi hāt; it must show that the cluster /st/ assimilated to the Maharashtri Prakrit geminate hattha, and that New Indo-Aryan degemination then lengthened the preceding vowel to give the modern form. Black-box neural systems cannot justify such steps. Language change also involves semantic drift, as words broaden, narrow, or shift metaphorically over centuries. Indic NLP initiatives such as AI4Bharat have produced valuable transliteration and translation resources [10], but diachronically aligned etymological corpora and models for Indic historical evolution are still lacking.
Research gap. No existing framework unites explicit historical sound-law knowledge, articulatory feature grounding, semantic drift modeling, and auditable derivation output for the Sanskrit-to-Marathi trajectory.
Objectives. This study aims to (i) formalize attested OIA→MIA→NIA sound laws as an executable rule engine; (ii) couple that engine with a phoneme-level Transformer over IPA articulatory features; (iii) model semantic drift between ancient etyma and modern reflexes; and (iv) produce human-auditable derivation traces.
Contributions. The main contributions are:
a rule-constrained cross-attention mechanism that penalizes phonotactically illegal transitions while retaining neural flexibility for irregular items;
an integrated architecture combining PanPhon articulatory features, a contrastive semantic drift objective, and a traceable derivation module;
a curated benchmark of 12,500 aligned Sanskrit–Marathi cognate pairs with intermediate Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa targets; and
an empirical evaluation with ablations showing consistent gains over symbolic and neural baselines, plus a planned open-source toolkit.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work, Section III presents the methodology, Section IV describes the system architecture, Section V details the implementation, Section VI reports results, Section VII discusses limitations, and Section VIII concludes.
II. Literature Review and Related Work
A. Comparative philology. The systematic study of Indo-Aryan diachronic phonology grew out of nineteenth- and twentieth-century comparative philology. Bloch’s La formation de la langue marathe established the phonetic correspondences that set Marathi apart from northern and eastern Indo-Aryan, including elision of intervocalic voiceless stops, reduction of sibilants, and retention of the retroflex lateral [2]. Turner’s Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages (CDIAL) cataloged thousands of cognate sets tracing Sanskrit roots through Prakrit intermediaries to modern languages [3], and Masica synthesized pan-Indic shifts in syllable weight, vowel length, and case syncretism [4]. These works remain descriptive and paper-bound: they offer no algorithm that predicts unseen derivations, validates proposed etymologies, or plugs into language technology.
B. Statistical and phylogenetic methods. Early computational work relied on alignment algorithms, edit-distance variants [11], and Bayesian phylogenetic trees that estimate divergence dates and cognate clusters [12]. These methods typically operate on binary cognate presence/absence matrices and therefore bypass the phonetic mechanics of sound change.
C. Neural proto-language reconstruction. Meloni et al. applied recurrent encoder-decoders with attention [13] to reconstruct Proto-Romance forms from modern reflexes [14]. Kim et al. moved to Transformer architectures for multi-daughter reflex alignment [15], and Lu et al. introduced the Daughter-to-Proto-to-Daughter (DPD) framework, which uses cycle-consistent reflex prediction to improve reconstruction under limited supervision [9]. Unconstrained Transformers, however, need large corpora; on small historical datasets they hallucinate sequences that violate the Neogrammarian regularity hypothesis [5], and treating phonemes as atomic tokens discards the physical properties of articulation.
D. Articulatory feature representations. Mortensen et al. introduced PanPhon, which maps more than 5,000 IPA segments to 24 articulatory subsegmental features [16]. PanPhon has supported phonologically structured modeling in dialectology and speech technology, but it has not previously been embedded in Transformer models for Indo-Aryan diachronic reconstruction.
E. Indic NLP. The Indic NLP ecosystem has matured through AI4Bharat, which released the Aksharantar transliteration corpus of roughly 26 million pairs, translation models, and benchmarks [10]. These resources focus almost exclusively on synchronic tasks in modern scheduled languages. Sanskrit tools concentrate on sandhi splitting, compound analysis, and parsing, while Marathi tools target tagging, sentiment, and named entity recognition. No existing framework bridges these epochs by modeling the phonological transitions and semantic drift that link Sanskrit roots to Marathi derivatives.

IV. System Architecture and Design
The system has four tiers: the Ingestion and Phonetic Feature Layer, the Linguistic Rule Formulation Layer, the Rule-Constrained Neural Core, and the Traceable Explanation Layer (Fig. 1).
Overall system architecture and data flow.
Ingestion and Phonetic Feature Layer. Raw Sanskrit Devanagari entries undergo Unicode NFC normalization to resolve virama and anusvara variants, are converted to IPA streams by a rule-based grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) converter for Classical Sanskrit, and are linked to 24-dimensional PanPhon vectors. Downstream modules therefore operate on phonetic features rather than orthography.
Linguistic Rule Formulation Layer. A compiled bank of deterministic finite-state rewrite rules applies the sound laws in sequence, simulating lenition, cluster assimilation, degemination, compensatory lengthening, and vowel shifts. It emits two outputs: intermediate Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa hypotheses, and the transition prior matrix  that supplies log-penalties to the decoder.
Rule-Constrained Neural Core. A multi-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder converts the articulatory-projected source into contextual vectors. An autoregressive decoder generates the Marathi phoneme sequence with cross-attention heads biased by . In parallel, a contrastive projection head computes semantic drift representations with the InfoNCE loss.
Traceable Explanation Layer. As the decoder selects phonemes, this layer records active attention weights and matches them to rule identifiers from the symbolic engine. The phoneme stream is converted to Modern Marathi Devanagari through an inverted G2P mapping, and the final output contains both the reflex and a derivation log of every intermediate stage and applied sound law.

V. Implementation
A. Technology stack. The framework is implemented in Python 3.11 with PyTorch 2.3 [19] and Hugging Face Transformers [20], using PyTorch Lightning for training management. Articulatory features come from the PanPhon library [16], and G2P conversion for Classical Sanskrit uses customized Epitran backends [21]. Devanagari–IAST transliteration and normalization use the IndicNLP Library [22]. Contrastive semantic embeddings are initialized from fastText [23] models trained on Sanskrit text collections and the Marathi IndicCorp dataset [24], then refined through learned linear projection heads. Cognate sets, rule tables, and derivations are stored in PostgreSQL with JSONB indexing.
B. Dataset. Because no machine-readable Sanskrit-to-Marathi diachronic dataset exists, a corpus was curated from Turner’s CDIAL [3], Bloch’s The Formation of the Marathi Language [2], and Molesworth’s Marathi-English Dictionary [25]. Fourth, each Sanskrit root is aligned with its Prakrit form, Apabhraṃśa reflex, and Modern Marathi derivative to give multi-stage targets.

VI. Results and Discussion
A. Metrics and baselines. Four metrics are reported: Character Error Rate (CER), the phoneme-level normalized Levenshtein distance between target and prediction; Phonetic Edit Distance (PED), computed with PanPhon’s articulatory feature distance to measure phonetic similarity [16]; Phoneme BLEU, the n-gram overlap of predicted phoneme sequences; and chrF++ [27], a character n-gram F-score suited to morphologically rich languages. Four baselines were used: (i) a deterministic rule FST engine without learning; (ii) a bidirectional LSTM with Bahdanau attention [13]; (iii) a vanilla 4-layer Transformer over discrete phonemes with no articulatory features or rule constraints; and (iv) an unconstrained reflex Transformer adapted from proto-form reconstruction work [9], [15].

B. Quantitative analysis. The full hybrid framework outperforms both symbolic and unconstrained neural baselines on every metric (Table IV, Fig. 2). The rule FST engine reaches 18.42% CER; it models regular changes accurately but fails on irregular items, loanwords, and late dialectal innovations. The vanilla Transformer lowers CER to 11.20% but keeps a high PED of 0.76 because it produces articulatorily distant phonemes. Against the strongest neural baseline, the unconstrained reflex Transformer, the full model cuts CER from 9.84% to 6.12%, an absolute reduction of 3.72 points (37.8% relative), and lowers PED from 0.62 to 0.41.

Setting  removes the contrastive drift loss and raises CER to 7.15%, indicating that semantic grounding helps choose among competing reflexes for polysemous roots. VII. Limitations
Several constraints remain. First, corpus scale: 12,500 cognate pairs is large for historical linguistics but modest by modern NLP standards, which limits resolution of rare lexical exceptions. Second, Marathi was shaped by contact with Dravidian languages, which contributed non-Indo-European deshya vocabulary and influenced retroflexion; because the rule engine encodes Indo-Aryan sound laws, accuracy drops on loanwords and substrate-influenced items. Third, semantic changes such as metaphorical extension and pejoration are non-deterministic and hard to capture with continuous embeddings alone. Fourth, building and applying rule constraint matrices during decoding adds latency compared with unconstrained Transformer inference. Finally, the random stratified split may let related root families appear in both training and test sets; evaluation on root-family-disjoint splits is needed to confirm generalization to truly unseen etymologies.
VIII. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper presented a hybrid artificial intelligence framework that reconstructs the diachronic evolution of Marathi lexical forms from Sanskrit roots, bridging traditional historical linguistics and neural sequence modeling. By combining a symbolic engine of Indo-Aryan sound laws with an IPA-grounded Transformer, the architecture addresses three failure modes of unconstrained neural models: phonological hallucination, lack of explainability, and poor generalization on low-resource historical corpora.
Integrating PanPhon articulatory features with rule-constrained cross-attention achieved a 6.12% CER and a 0.41 PED on a curated corpus of 12,500 cognate pairs, outperforming all baselines, and ablations confirmed that both the rule bias and the semantic drift objective contribute. The framework also emits interpretable derivation traces that show intermediate Maharashtri Prakrit and Apabhraṃśa stages with the rule identifiers applied.
Future work will pursue four directions. First, the architecture will be extended to a multi-task phylogenetic model that traces the divergence of sister New Indo-Aryan languages, including Gujarati, Hindi, Konkani, and Bengali, from shared MIA etyma. Second, cycle-consistency objectives between daughter reflexes and ancestral forms [9] will support unsupervised recovery of unattested intermediate dialects without exhaustive gold annotations. Third, the engine will be interfaced with optical character recognition to help epigraphers restore and interpret damaged Modi-script and Devanagari inscriptions and manuscripts. Finally, the dataset, rule definitions, and pretrained models will be released as an open-source Python library for Indic computational historical linguistics and cultural preservation.
